Before and after

A different sliceof Japan

My first trip to Japan was Tokyo. It looked like the Japan I knew from films and anime: futuristic, fast, always hustling. This time I wanted to find something different.

Osaka and Kyoto gave me a different slice of life: people resting by a river, a tea shop in the rain, a village where boats sit under houses, a shopping street where every shop has a face. The itinerary ran Osaka near Namba, Kyoto near Gion, Uji between the two, and a night in Ine.

The longer I looked at ordinary things, the more I noticed what stood behind them.

01 · Attention · Osaka

Attention

A clear signal wins the first look. A claim people can check earns the second.

Signs

A street full of identities

Osaka · Storefronts, characters, and being remembered

Looking back through my Osaka photos, I noticed how often I had photographed a sign. Before reading a menu, I already had something to remember. Browsing became part of the experience, and each specialty became a character with a place in the city’s visual identity.

Learning

An identity you can recognize at a glance

Arts & branding

Each sign gives people one clear visual to recognize and remember: the crab names the specialty, the bull gives it a personality, the Glico runner makes a pose memorable. None of them asks the passer-by to read first.

Choice

Why “No. 1” helps people choose

Learning

Social proof works because people assume it was earned

A rank borrows the crowd’s confidence

“No. 1” was everywhere in Osaka: on drinks, on billboards, even on job ads. It answers a question every newcomer has - is this worth trying? - by pointing to everyone who already chose it.

A standard beats a rank

A rank says many people chose it. At a Kobe beef restaurant, the reassurance said someone checked it. A diner cannot inspect the animal, but can trust a rule that a third party publishes and enforces.

02 · Attachment · Osaka & Uji

Attachment

People travel for what they already love. The same pull can turn into an obligation.

Fans

Why a backdrop can be worth the journey

Osaka · Supporting a favorite · A shared moment

I wanted to understand why fans would gather around a backdrop to take pictures, even when the idol was not there. The image offers a place to express an attachment people already have. Choosing a pose or taking a photo with friends turns admiration into a personal memory. The backdrop is simple; the feeling people bring to it is not.

Learning

Idol culture: making support visible

Culture · Supporting a favorite

An oshi is a favorite person or character; oshikatsu is the activity around supporting them. Listening, attending, collecting, and sharing can make that attachment part of everyday life.

Fun fact · Oshi no Ko

The title means “my oshi’s child.” Author Aka Akasaka built it on a fan joke: when an idol announces a marriage, fans say they want to be reborn as that idol’s child. In the story, a doctor is reborn as the secret son of idol Ai Hoshino, who hides her twins to protect her image.

Its opening song, YOASOBI’s “Idol,” became the first song originally in Japanese to top Billboard’s Global Excl. U.S. chart, in June 2023. “Idol” was also one of our own winning ad creatives.

The secrecy reflects a real rule: many idol contracts ban dating. In 2015 a Tokyo court ordered a 17-year-old former idol to pay ¥650,000 for dating; in 2016 the same court rejected a ¥9.9 million claim in a similar case, saying the ban restricts the freedom to pursue happiness.

What makes Japanese idol fandom different

Fans everywhere stream, buy, and travel. Japanese idol fandom adds rituals that let one fan show, in public, exactly whom they support - and get noticed for it.

メンバーカラー · Member color
A color for every member

Each member owns a color, and fans switch their penlight to their oshi’s. The hall shows at a glance how support splits. Momoiro Clover Z made colors central from 2010; penlights go back to 1974, when Saijo Hideki asked fans to bring “something that glows.” K-pop crowds more often hold one official lightstick per group, synced by the show.

うちわ · Fan sign
A hand fan that asks for a moment
ピースして

Fans letter their own uchiwa with requests - “do a peace sign,” “wave at me” - hoping for fansa, a moment of fan service aimed at them. Organizers even regulate their size and how high they are held.

会いに行ける · Idols you can meet
Contact is part of the product
  1. Buy a CD
  2. Get a ticket or ballot
  3. Seconds of contact, or a vote

AKB48 launched in 2005 as “idols you can go and meet,” with near-daily theater shows in Akihabara. Handshake events and instant-photo sessions turn a purchase into a few seconds of contact, and fan votes rank the members.

  1. Let people pick their oshi. A creative that asks the viewer to choose a member, a color, or a song turns an ad into an act of support. Our “Idol” creative already showed the pull.
  2. Don’t stop at teens. Nearly half of supporters are 40+: test older age bands and the media they use.
  3. Collabs are the thin slice. Collaboration spending is the smallest part of the market (about ¥0.3T), so licensed songs and IP tie-ins still have room to stand out.

Love for an idol becomes visible when there is somewhere to share it - even a poster in a shopping street.
Stories

From a real place
to a world we love

Uji · Anime pilgrimage · Sound! Euphonium

Artists borrow the texture of everyday life: a railing, a station, a bench beside a river. A story gives those details an emotional role. Put the scene beside the place and you can see both the observation and the transformation.

Learning

Observe the place. Shape the feeling. Give people a reason to return.

Uji · Walking into Sound! Euphonium

The series follows a school concert band, but much of its emotional world lives between rehearsals: by the river, on the way home, in places where characters reflect. That is why a small everyday spot can become a destination for someone who knows the story.

Uji has embraced the series

More than ten years after the first broadcast, Uji and its railway still treat Sound! Euphonium as part of the town. Keihan’s autumn 2026 collaboration alone runs from 1 September to 20 December.

Train, map, and bus images: Keihan Electric Railway, official collaboration site. Vending machine and platform: my trip.

A bench by a river is ordinary until a story sits on it. After that, people cross the country to sit there too.
Limits

When attention is someone’s job

Osaka · Host and hostess culture · Where attachment becomes obligation

Learning

Host and hostess culture: a livelihood built around attention

The billboard sells a person; behind it is a job

The billboards make the invitation visible: a face, a mood, the promise of a special evening. Behind it are people earning a living by making customers feel welcome and remembered. This is the same pull as fandom, with money and a working relationship in the middle.

A novel and an anime, two lenses
The quiet side: a job that supports a family

In Keigo Higashino’s The Devotion of Suspect X, Yasuko’s hostess work supported her and her daughter, and the network around it later gave her a different job. The novel shows the judgment that follows her: about age, respectability, and her daughter’s future. She reads less as a glamorous figure than as a working mother with limited options.

The playful side: hosting as a performed character

Ouran High School Host Club (Bisco Hatori’s manga, anime 2006) parodies the business. Rich students run a “host club” where each member plays a type for his guests: Tamaki the princely type, Kyoya the cool type, Mori the wild type. Haruhi joins to work off an ¥8 million debt for a broken vase. The comedy works because the formula is familiar: a persona chosen to match what the customer wants, and a debt that keeps someone in the job.

Osaka and Tokyo: where the nightlife lives

Today’s nightlife districts trace back to Edo-era licensed quarters, where the shogunate confined prostitution to one district per city - Shinmachi in Osaka, Shimabara in Kyoto, Yoshiwara in Edo.

Banned, but not gone. The 1956 Prostitution Prevention Act outlaws only intercourse for payment, so much of the sex trade runs legally as licensed businesses. Host and hostess clubs sit further from the line: they are licensed to sell drinks, conversation, and attention.

Osaka

Kitashinchi began as an Edo-era pleasure quarter used by rice merchants and warehouse officials. After the war it became Osaka’s upscale bar-and-club district, nicknamed “the chamber of commerce of the night” for the business done there.

Tokyo

Kabukicho is the center of host clubs. The first host club is said to be Night Tokyo, opened in 1965 near Tokyo Station; Takeshi Aida’s Club Ai, opened in 1971, set the model for today’s Kabukicho clubs.

What it offers - and where the costs fall

Akiko Takeyama’s fieldwork, Staged Seduction, shows hosts and customers pursuing different hopes inside the same commercial relationship: for one, recognition and romance; for the other, income and a career. The encounter is repeatable, and the worker’s livelihood depends on the customer returning.

What people may gain

Workers: income, a customer network, and a way to turn social skill into earnings.

Customers: entertainment, recognition, companionship, and a familiar place to return.

What can become harmful

Workers: pressure to keep performing intimacy, insecure prospects, stigma, and blurred boundaries.

Customers: spending that is hard to control, debt, and pressure to keep supporting a favorite.

A 2025 revision of the entertainment business law banned exploiting customers’ romantic feelings to sell drinks and misleading explanations of charges. Police also told clubs that billboards hyping a host’s sales or “No. 1” rank were no longer acceptable; in Kabukicho, clubs covered the slogans with black tape. In 2025, police inspected 1,083 of the roughly 1,100 host clubs nationwide and issued 251 administrative penalties.

The same “No. 1” that sells a drink on a bottle was, on a host billboard, judged to push people into debt.

Attention can be meaningful to the person receiving it and also be someone else’s livelihood. Both can be true in the same room.
Taste

The pleasure Japan ranks first

Osaka and the whole trip · Everyday food, away from the omakase counter

From outside, Japan’s nightlife and adult industry can make sex look like the national pleasure. Ask people in Japan, and food comes first.

Osaka has a word for it: kuidaore, eating until you drop. These are the everyday meals of the trip.

A stereotype tells you what a place is known for. Asking its people tells you what they enjoy.
03 · Depth · Uji, Osaka & Kyoto

Depth

A familiar entry point, a whole world behind it, and meanings that keep changing.

01

Matcha

Uji · Tea culture · What makes a specialty special

One familiar drink, a whole world behind it

Uji in the rain, with my favorite matcha drink, remains one of my strongest memories. Behind that familiar taste were leaves, processing methods, utensils, and people who could recognize differences I was only beginning to notice. A specialty can look narrow from outside and contain a whole world within it.

Learning

Matcha: the depth behind a familiar name

Why Uji? The history is real

Matcha is ground tencha, a tea grown in shade. Uji’s reputation comes from developing that shading method and from five centuries of patronage by the people who set Japan’s taste in tea.

Is it still true? Partly

Today “Uji tea” is a regional brand, not one town’s fields. It means tea grown in Kyoto, Nara, Shiga, or Mie and finished by Kyoto Prefecture companies using methods that originated in Uji. The name guarantees a finishing craft and a standard, not that the leaf grew beside the river.

The standing is also being tested. Kyoto Prefecture’s own 2024 tea-industry review notes that tencha output is growing fast in other regions, and that Kyoto must set its tea apart, including by replanting with Uji cultivars.

The world found the doorway

Japan’s green tea exports reached ¥72.1 billion in 2025, nearly double the year before, led by powdered tea such as matcha used in lattes and desserts. The latte is the entry point; Uji is the history behind it.

02

The Art of Omakase

Osaka · YUGEN · Chef Keisuke Mifune

Leave the choice to the chef, and the season comes to you

At YUGEN I only chose my drinks. The chef had already decided the season’s courses, and the meal felt like a flower garden: ingredients, vessels, and pace made the changing season something I could notice before tasting.

Learning

Omakase: trusting someone’s craft

Culture

Omakase (お任せ) comes from makaseru, “to entrust”: the guest leaves the choice to the chef. The habit began at Edo-era sushi stalls, which had no menus, and spread after the war through high-end counters in Ginza and Nihonbashi, where guests relied on the chef to pick the day’s best fish. YUGEN serves a set kaiseki course, ten courses at dinner, built on the same trust.

Arts & craft

What the guest gives up, the chef uses. Chef Mifune visits farms to choose produce, changes the vessels with the season, and cooks the rice in a clay donabe. Six of the twelve seats face him across a single slab of hinoki, so the craft happens in front of the guest.

03

Kimono

Kyoto · A day in kimono with my team

An outfit that tells you where you stand

Walking in kimono with my team made the pace of the day part of the memory. The research afterward added another layer: similar-looking garments can communicate very different occasions, life stages, and relationships.

Learning

Reading the garment, the wearer, and the occasion

“Traditional” does not mean unchanged

The familiar form developed from the kosode, a straight-seamed robe that moved from undergarment to outer dress and, by the sixteenth century, was worn across classes and by both sexes. Its meaning kept shifting with law, money, and fashion.

Hierarchy · Class, wealth, and knowing the code

In Edo Japan, dress was regulated. The Met describes merchant women using fashion to display wealth despite restrictions: a garment could signal resources and taste while the wearer’s legal class remained something else. Modern formality is a different hierarchy: how an ensemble fits the event and the wearer’s role, not a ranking of worth.

The code still matters at the top. In Kyoto I saw women wearing kimono to business meetings, and in public life it marks the moments that represent Japan.

What this has meant for women

Kimono can make expectations about age, marriage, and femininity publicly visible. Ofra Goldstein-Gidoni’s 1999 study argues that modern Japan made women in kimono carriers of national tradition, while men were associated with modern achievement. Women have also used clothing to express independence: NGV describes young women choosing their own fashions in the modern city and designer Hiroko Takahashi making bold unisex kimono. Obligation, pleasure, and experimentation can coexist.

How it felt: slower, softer, tightly bound

Kimono constrains the body. The narrow wrap shortens every step, the cords and layers press on the ribs until breathing turns shallow, and everything slows down. From the outside, that reads as softness.

A Vietnamese saying turns that look into an ideal: “Ăn cơm Tàu, ở nhà Tây, lấy vợ Nhật” - eat Chinese food, live in a Western house, marry a Japanese wife. The wife in that picture is gentle, devoted, and deferential, the posture the kimono trains. Behind the image is a real gap:

118th

of 148 countries in the World Economic Forum’s 2025 Gender Gap Index.

Last

in the G7, with the same rank as the year before.

125th

on political empowerment, down from 113th, with two women in a cabinet of twenty.

The whole outfit carries meaning

Obi, collar, sleeve length, and accessories shape the message; seasonal flowers mark timing. Wrapping accommodates floor-seated life, as the V&A notes.

A garment can tell you the occasion, the relationship, and the life stage before anyone speaks. Reading it well means knowing the code - and knowing the code changes.

People & place

Every view I enjoyed was also someone’s place of worship, workplace, or home.

04

Temples & Prayers

Kyoto · Practice inside the place

Understanding the practice inside the place

Kyoto’s temples and shrines are not museums. School groups were everywhere, being shown how to pray, and nearly every gate sold charms for exams, business, love, and safe travel. The belief is carried less by doctrine than by habit - and the habit starts young.

Learning

Kyoto: places of worship, gestures of hope

750,000

school-trip students stayed in Kyoto in 2024 - about a quarter of Japan’s 3.04 million students in school-trip grades.

17

UNESCO-listed sites in historic Kyoto: 13 temples, 3 shrines, and Nijō Castle.

36%

of Japanese say they follow a religion - yet the rituals stay part of ordinary life.

Learned young, on the road

Japan’s school trip dates back to 1886, when Tokyo Normal School took its students on a long-distance excursion. Kyoto became its classic destination. The city’s tourism association gives every student group a “school trip passport” listing temples, shrines, and workshops - some take bookings for zazen meditation and sutra copying. On the way, children learn the gestures on site: where to purify, when to bow, and when not to clap.

Belief through practice, not membership

Few people call themselves religious, but the rituals mark a whole life. Families bring children to a shrine at ages 3, 5, and 7 for Shichi-go-san. Students pray at Kitano Tenmangu, the shrine of the scholar Sugawara no Michizane, before exams. Businesses pray for prosperity at Fushimi Inari, head of some 30,000 Inari shrines nationwide, under gates that carry their donors’ names.

A charm for every worry

Omamori (お守り) are protective charms, each made for one concern; ema are wooden tablets where people write a wish or a thanks.

  • 学業成就Study and exams
  • 商売繁盛Business prosperity
  • 縁結びLove and good matches
  • 交通安全Safe travel
  • 健康Health
  • 安産Safe childbirth

They are not meant to be kept forever. After about a year, or once the wish comes true, people return them to a shrine box, and the shrine burns them in a sacred fire.

Fortune slips (omikuji) follow the same logic: a small offering in exchange for guidance. At Nishiki Tenmangu, a shrine to Sugawara no Michizane - whose messenger is the cow - a coin sets a mechanical lion dancer moving, and it carries your fortune out.

Kennin-ji: where the tea story starts

Kennin-ji, near Gion, calls itself Kyoto’s oldest Zen temple. It was founded in 1202 with Eisai as its first abbot - the monk the Uji tea history credits with bringing tea seeds from China. The ink twin dragons across its ceiling (Koizumi Junsaku, 2002, for the 800th anniversary) and its Wind God and Thunder God screen are what most visitors come for.

The temple also offers seated meditation, chanting, and sutra copying: ways of giving time and attention rather than collecting an object. I read about these rather than joined them.

Understanding a place means learning what people do there, what they hope for, and why the gestures matter.
Streets

Beauty kept on purpose

Kyoto · Townhouses, street rules, and the seasons

Kyoto’s old streets look effortless. They are not: the city rules on what a building, a sign, or a demolition may do, and residents keep adjusting houses that were designed around light, air, and the street.

Learning

A city that protects its skyline and its houses

2007

Kyoto’s landscape policy reset building heights citywide and banned rooftop signs and flashing or moving signs everywhere.

~2 a day

Machiya still disappear: from 40,146 in 2016 to 34,580 in 2024.

1 year

Notice owners must give before demolishing a protected machiya, under a 2017 ordinance.

Four design ideas inside a machiya
Lattices handle privacy and identity

Kōshi let in light and air while hiding the room from the street. Their pattern once told you the trade inside: thread shops cut the top to let in light for judging cloth, sake shops used thick bars that survive a knock from a barrel, and charcoal sellers kept the gaps tight to hold in dust.

What I read as style was also function - and a shop sign.

A small garden is climate control

The tsuboniwa, a pocket courtyard, pulls light and air into a long, narrow house and gives every room a view of the season.

Beauty and usefulness were the same decision.

The edge belongs to the street

Deep eaves and fold-down battari shōgi benches make a semi-public strip for displaying goods or resting. At festivals, families take the lattices off.

A house contributes to the street through the space at its edge.

The house changes clothes for summer

In summer, paper sliding doors give way to reed screens, bamboo blinds, and rattan mats.

I saw one version of the house. Living in it means re-dressing it every season.

Artists build the season into the work

Arashiyama Yusai-tei, a Meiji-era house on the site of a palace from about 800 years ago, is designed around the maples outside. Round windows frame them and a polished table mirrors them, so the room becomes a new work each season. Owner and dyer Okuda Yusai works the same way: his Yume-kōro dye, developed from the emperor’s kōrozen color, turns a burning red when held up to sunlight.

05

Kifune

Sacred water · A shrine shared with many visitors

The art of making a place into a brand

Kifune left me with mixed feelings. I had hoped for more space to reflect, yet I was struck by how creatively the area turned water, landscape, and local associations into products. A “full-moon water” story stayed with me: both cosmetics and a craft beer tied their ingredients to Kifune.

Learning

Kifune: a place becomes a brand story

Culture

Kifune’s water and rain associations include homage by farmers and sake brewers. Kyoto’s historical account places commerce and livelihood inside a long religious history: selling the water’s story is not new.

Branding · A place, an ingredient, and a moment

KIFUNE COSMETICS began with cosmetics made from local water drawn on a full-moon night. The beer menu tells the same story through Kifune spring water, Belgian yeast, and yuzu. A specific place and ritual turn an everyday ingredient into something people can remember and take home.

06

Ine

A night in a funaya · A morning bicycle ride

Seeing the work inside the view

In Ine, I stayed in a funaya, a boathouse with a room above the water. A morning bicycle ride and simple greetings stayed with me alongside the waterfront. I was moving through a place where people were going about their lives. Learning how the boathouses supported fishing changed the view: their beauty carries the shape of work, and the village continues beyond the experience I came to enjoy.

Learning

Ine: the work behind the view

Architecture: a garage below, a room above

Each funaya is built around a boat. The ground floor is a garage whose floor slopes into the sea, so a boat can be hauled straight in; the second floor is extra living space or storage. The family’s main house stands behind it, across the narrow road on the hillside. About 230 line 5 km of shore, gable ends to the water, clad in charred cedar under dark tile.

Why only here: a bay that barely moves

Ine Bay sits on the Sea of Japan, yet it opens to the south and a small island partly shields its mouth. Waves stay calm all year, the tide moves about 30 cm a day, and the sea level only about 70 cm across the year. That is what lets a house stand with its floor in the sea.

From thatched sheds to two-storey homes

Villagers first lived midway up the slopes and moved down to the shore in the 18th century. The first funaya were single-storey and thatched, tall enough to hang fishing nets. Later rebuilds added a second floor and tiled roofs; the oldest standing funaya dates from 1863.

Sleeping above the boats

A few owners have rebuilt funaya inside as modern whole-house rentals, so guests sleep above the bay. The town separates stays from meals: alongside the rentals, it has built facilities with restaurants and cafés. Most funaya remain private homes and workplaces, and the tourism association reminds visitors that Ine is a residential village.

Protected as a working place

In 2005, Ine-ura became the first fishing village selected as a national Important Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings, and the first to include the sea within its boundary. What is protected is not a single monument but a way of living with the water.

The cost of being loved

Attention has a price that residents often pay first. Kyoto raised its lodging-tax ceiling to ¥10,000 per person per night from March 2026, as the city responds to visitor pressure. Kifune’s crowds and Ine’s request for quiet are smaller versions of the same question: who carries the cost of a place becoming famous?

A beautiful view can also be someone’s workplace and home. Enjoying it well means knowing whose it is.
What I brought home

Beyond the
First Look

Japan showed me how fast I form an impression, and how much longer it takes to understand what sits behind it.

Most of what stayed with me came after the first look: why a tradition continued, who keeps a place running, what a famous view costs the people who live there. I want to keep looking deeper and exploring past my first impression.

Start with motivation. In UA, the first impression is the part we can buy. Before each brief, I want to understand the audience’s reason to care, from reviews, conversations, and community language.

If I did it again
Make, not only lookJoin a workshop to understand the arts and crafts from the inside. Yusai-tei, where I photographed the round windows, is also dyer Okuda Yusai’s studio for his own Yume-kōro dyeing technique.
Skip KifunePick a less crowded, less touristy place, or check TikTok before going.
豆知識 · Fun fact

No license needed, but a test is

Since July 2023, anyone 16 or older can ride an e-scooter in Japan without a driver’s license, capped at 20 km/h. The check moved into the app instead: LUUP, the shared scooters on many city corners, makes every rider answer its traffic-rules test correctly, every question in a row, before the first ride. I had to pass it too.
